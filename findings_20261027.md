# Post-eMudhra review findings — 2026-10-27

A security review of the whole tree at commit `9a60643` (ledger, signing, consensus, node, wallet,
config and docs), prompted by the eMudhra weak-key incident, and the hardening that followed the
same day. The full report, with file and line references to the reviewed tree, is
[`review/post_emudhra_review_2026-10-27.md`](review/post_emudhra_review_2026-10-27.md). The
proof-of-concept tests, harnesses, run logs and prototype fixes are in
[`review/_evidence_2026-10-27/`](review/_evidence_2026-10-27/README.md). This file records what was
found, what happened to each finding, and what is still open.

Identifiers in this file use the spelled-out names from the rename commit "names: spell out
variable, type and method names", and [`review/renames_2026-10-27.md`](review/renames_2026-10-27.md)
maps the old names to the new ones.

The review and the fixes were made by LLM agents and checked by other agents. Like the rounds in
[`findings.md`](findings.md), they are inputs to an independent human audit, not a substitute for
one (see [`docs/why-small-and-auditable.md`](docs/why-small-and-auditable.md)).

---

## Why this review

In October 2026 eight eMudhra intermediate CA keys turned out to hold only 3–8 bits of entropy. A
key-ceremony script error had produced a weak second key set, which was then used by mistake. The
keys could be recovered from the public certificates, and they stayed exposed for about 17 months.

The review asked two questions of go-ledger in that light. Could the same failure happen here? And
does the owner's rule, "two different signings means the key is burned", catch a leaked or weak
key?

## How it was run

Three passes, each by independent agents working on private copies of the tree:

1. **Find.** Nine reviewers, one per area: key lifecycle, the burn rule, the consensus core, the
   ledger state transition, governance, sync and trust anchors, the node's input boundary, the
   wallet, and the flaky test plus the full `-race` suite. They reported 53 findings, merged into
   36 clusters.
2. **Verify.** Eight groups tried to refute every cluster by re-running or rewriting the proofs of
   concept through the production code paths, and a completeness critic looked for what was
   missed. Two hypotheses from the lead were added. Result: 35 confirmed, 2 plausible, 1 refuted,
   and 19 new findings.
3. **Verify the new ones.** Five more groups re-checked the 19 new findings with their own tests,
   several on real multi-process networks running `cmd/server`. All 19 held up. Two duplicated
   others, and one was a limit of a suggested fix rather than of the code. This pass also showed
   that two suggested fixes do not work: a signer guard alone does not stop the E2 fork, and
   pooling a governance proposal once one vote verifies does not stop E4.

Findings with one root cause were merged, which gives 45. Severity follows the threat model in
`docs/security.md`: one dishonest validator, anyone on the LAN, sleeping or rebooting laptops, and
operator mistakes.

- **Critical:** one in-scope party routinely moves or destroys other people's money.
- **High:** a fork, a permanent halt, lost money or names, or a network-wide stall under realistic
  conditions.
- **Medium:** the same harms under narrower conditions, or an outage that recovers.
- **Low:** local or unlikely.
- **Info:** no harm today.

Baseline: `go build` and `go vet` were clean on both modules, and the full suite, chaos scenarios
included, passed twice under `-race`. `TestPaymentGossipReceiptAndReplay` failed about 1 run in 8;
the cause was a real liveness bug (E14), not test timing.

## Bottom line

- **45 weak points:** 1 critical, 11 high, 20 medium, 10 low and 3 informational. Two of them (E36,
  E45) are rated plausible rather than confirmed. One more claim was refuted.
- **The eMudhra failure cannot happen here.** Every key-generation path (`signing`, `cmd/keygen`,
  the wallet, `cmd/demo`) draws from `crypto/rand`. The wallet's at-rest sealing checks out against
  the standard library on 300 random vectors. The key-handling gaps found were in loading and
  storing keys, not in generating them (E26, E27, E37).
- **The burn rule did not hold.** Only consensus votes were checked for conflicts, and even then a
  slash deleted the offender's account while its key could keep voting (E13). Two different
  proposals for one round went unnoticed (E16), and payments, account updates and governance votes
  have no conflict slot at all (E43, E44). An honest validator that rebooted mid-height signed
  against its own earlier votes (E2), so a strict rule would have burned honest validators.
- **The worst three:**
  - a single validator could re-apply payments from earlier blocks, including payments to itself
    (E1);
  - a validator restart could fork the chain with no dishonest validator involved (E2);
  - in dynamic-validator mode, nodes trusted the genesis keys forever, so a restart could silently
    roll a node back to genesis and a node that fell behind never caught up (E3).
- **Anyone who could reach a node's port** could freeze governance for good (E4), stall a node for
  seconds per message (E6), or halt the chain with free account updates (E8). The planned edge proxy
  does not remove these, because it must forward the wallet and admin routes they use. (2026-11-01:
  the fetcher, built in Phase 3, passes on payments, updates and name requests only. The
  certificates reach a node only through the gossip port, behind its allow list, or the operator
  port, which is never published.)
- **What happened:** 38 findings are fixed in Phase 1, two of them only in part, each with a
  regression test. Five wait for the new genesis of Phase 2, one is documented and one is accepted.
  Phase 2, one new genesis built in slices S1 to S10 and S7b by 2026-10-30, fixes those five and
  finishes the two fixed in part; each is marked below with its slice.
  An adversarial review of the fixes confirmed 24 more issues, one of them high, and 23 are fixed.
  Three chaos tests long dismissed as flaky turned out to be production liveness bugs with two
  causes. Both are fixed at the tests' loss rate; under heavy message loss a rare residual is still
  left to checkpoint catch-up (see the limits at the end).

## Findings and what happened to each

Phase 1 fixes the code as it stands and needs no new genesis: snapshots, wallet files and
signatures stay valid. A fix marked *rule* changes what makes a block valid, so every validator must
switch to it at the same time; the others can roll out node by node. Phase 2 holds what changes the
state digest, a signed format or the JSON form, and starts from one new genesis.

Each Phase 1 fix is a commit whose subject starts with the finding numbers it fixes (for example
"E1: reject proposals whose block is labelled with another height"). Corrections made after the
adversarial review end in "(review follow-up)", and the last two E2 commits end in "(follow-up)".
Every finding fix came with a regression test that fails without it.

**Fixed in Phase 1, with regression tests**

- **E1 (CRITICAL): a block's height was never checked.** The scheduled proposer could label its
  block with an old height and fill it with payments signed for that height, including payments to
  itself, and every honest node applied them again. A node now treats a block labelled with another
  height as invalid and never stores it, and `commit` refuses one. Phase 2 also puts the height in
  the ledger state, so `ApplyBlock` enforces it for every caller. The `findings.md` #7 note that
  this was "not currently exploitable" was wrong. **Phase 2 part fixed (S2):** `LedgerState.Height`
  is in the state digest, and `ApplyBlock` refuses any block but the previous height plus one.
- **E2 (HIGH): a restarted validator forgot its own votes.** A validator's lock, round and votes
  lived only in memory, so a reboot mid-height could fork the chain with no dishonest validator
  involved (5 of 5 runs in each schedule tried), halt it, or make the validator sign evidence
  against itself. Each own vote is now written to a sign log at `<snapshotPath>.votes`, and fsynced,
  before it is counted or sent; nothing goes out if the write fails, and a restart at the same
  height resumes from the log. A validator must set `snapshotPath`. Three review follow-ups and two
  later follow-ups close the gaps found since (see below).
- **E3 (HIGH): dynamic mode trusted the genesis keys forever.** A node accepted a checkpoint, its
  own snapshot included, only if more than a third of genesis power had signed it. Once the
  founders paid out, a restarted node silently fell back to genesis (the chain rolled back 7 blocks
  after a simultaneous restart), a node that missed one commit never caught up, and old founder keys
  holding more than a third of genesis power could forge a joiner's starting state. A node now
  trusts its own snapshot when it is self-consistent, refuses to start if the file is there but
  does not verify, and anchors catch-up on its last committed accounts. A joiner still trusts the
  checkpoint it is seeded with (see the limits at the end).
- **E4 (HIGH): anyone on the port could freeze governance for good.** The parameter and
  reassignment pools created an entry before checking any vote, so 128 small unsigned POSTs per
  node filled both 64-entry pools, and every real parameter change and reassignment was refused.
  Nodes now pool only complete certificates that are valid against the current state. The wallet's
  admin page signs votes off the network and submits a complete certificate once.
- **E5 (HIGH, rule): a claw-back could be dodged by a same-block payment, and the dodge minted
  money.** A reassignment's target could pay its balance away in the enacting block; the payment
  went through, the reassignment still set the balance, and the supply grew. The enacting block now
  carries no payment from or to the reassigned account, and `ApplyBlock` rejects a block that does.
- **E6 (HIGH): exponent-form amounts cost seconds and megabytes before any check.** Amounts were
  decoded with exponents allowed, so 11 bytes (`"1p10000000"`) became a 10-million-bit number, and
  one unauthenticated 521-byte governance vote stalled a node's event loop for 8.5 s. Every amount
  now goes through one parser that refuses exponents and overlong text before `big.Rat` sees it.
  Phase 2 makes plain decimals the JSON form. **Phase 2 part fixed (S2):** every amount is written
  as one canonical plain decimal, in JSON and in signed fields, and read only as 1 to 18 digits
  with up to 8 decimals (`ledger.ParseAmount`, `ledger.FormatAmount`).
- **E7 (HIGH, rule): amounts had unbounded precision, which slowed every node.** After 1,000
  payments with large coprime denominators (100 units of fees), a balance took 1.56 MB, and one
  ordinary payment to that account took 14.1 s on every node. Every amount must now be a whole
  number of 10⁻⁸ units, checked at intake, by the proposer and in `ApplyBlock`. **Phase 2 (S2):**
  the parser also bounds the digits, so every amount is a plain decimal below 10^18 units.
- **E8 (HIGH): there was no block size limit.** About 545 free account updates of 1.9 KB each
  pushed every honest proposal for a height past the 1 MiB that peers decode, so the chain halted. A
  proposer now stops adding messages at 768 KiB of content, payments first, and carries at most one
  update per account per block. This is proposer policy, not a validity rule.
- **E9 (HIGH, rule): names were freed by balance events and renames.** Emptying an account, the
  overdraw ban, a slash or a rename released its name, so a thief with a stolen key, or anyone
  watching, could claim the name and receive every later payment to it. A named account is no
  longer deleted by a balance event, and an update may set a name only on an account that has none.
  Renames wait for Phase 2's ⅔ name action. **Phase 2 part fixed (S3, S5):** names are the account
  identity and an update sets no name (S3); a name moves to a new key only by a key change with its
  bank, or by the ⅔ name action (S5).
- **E10 (HIGH): DNS rebinding got past the wallet's origin check.** A web page that rebound its own
  hostname to 127.0.0.1 sent matching `Origin` and `Host` headers, so it could get payments and
  governance votes signed with the local key. The wallet now accepts only requests addressed to its
  own host and port, requires an `Origin` or `Referer` on a POST, and forbids framing.
- **E11 (HIGH): peer addresses published by only some validators partitioned the network.**
  Validators with an on-chain address sent only to each other, so once two or more of them held
  between a third and two thirds of the power, no block could commit. A real `cmd/server` network
  froze at height 6, against 82 for the control. Peers are now resolved from the chain only when
  every current validator has an address, and the configured peers are restored otherwise.
- **E12 (HIGH): one proposer could flood every node with proposals.** A validator could make every
  honest node apply, store and relay any number of different proposals for its rounds, far-future
  rounds included, at about 0.3 s of event-loop time per 1 MiB proposal. A node now takes only the
  first proposal per round whose block applies, for rounds up to the driver's round + 1. A review
  follow-up stops it checking a round once two proposals for it have failed to apply.
- **E14 (MEDIUM): proposals in the post-commit window were dropped.** A proposal for the next
  height that arrived between a commit and the next height's start was checked against the old
  height's proposer and dropped, so the round was lost, and a stale timer could fire into the next
  height. This was the "flaky" `TestPaymentGossipReceiptAndReplay`. The node now holds the next
  height's first proposals and its votes in that window, and every timer carries its height. On the
  reviewed code 22 of 40 runs of that test stalled for 3.6 to 20.6 s against its 20 s deadline; with
  the fix all 40 take under 2 s.
- **E17 (MEDIUM): votes were stored and relayed before they were checked.** Anyone on the port
  could make every node store junk votes without limit and re-gossip them. A node now keeps, relays
  and counts only votes its current height counts, and at most two per validator, round and type.
- **E18 (MEDIUM): a voter who left the set spoiled a certificate.** In dynamic mode one vote from a
  validator that had since left the set invalidated a whole governance certificate, so ordinary
  churn, or a griefer, could block a change that a live quorum supported. Votes of departed
  validators are now set aside when a certificate is picked and, after a review follow-up, at
  intake too.
- **E19 (MEDIUM, rule): a ⅔ vote could set timings that halt the chain for good.** Timings had only
  lower bounds, so a ratified block interval of 86,400,000,000 ms stopped the chain, and a revert
  needs a block. The block interval and the step timeout are now capped at one day, the longest
  window the documented pigeon mode uses.
- **E21 (MEDIUM): far-future payments and updates sat in the pools forever.** Intake put no upper
  bound on a message's `BlockID`, admitted payments below the fee, and took updates from keys with no
  account, so a fresh key could fill the 10,000-slot update pool, and every honest registration was
  answered 202 and dropped. A payment or update must now name a block at most 64 past the current
  height; payments below the fee and updates from keys with no account are refused, and stale
  entries are evicted. A review follow-up limits an account to one pending update per block.
- **E22 (MEDIUM): the state digest could not tell a missing amount from zero.** A relaying
  peer could null out a zero balance in a genuine checkpoint and keep its digest; the node then
  panicked on the next payment to that account, and again after every restart. A nil amount now
  hashes differently from zero. No honest state holds nil, so no committed digest changes.
- **E23 (MEDIUM): checkpoint fetches had no size limit.** One dishonest catch-up source could make a
  node decode an unbounded reply on every poll; with exponent amounts, a 4 KB reply cost a running
  node about 200 MB. A fetch now refuses a non-200 answer and reads at most 64 MiB.
- **E24 (MEDIUM, rule): one over-balance payment destroyed the payer's whole balance.** Honest
  intake refuses a payment larger than the payer's balance, but a dishonest validator that kept one
  could put it in a block and trigger the overdraw ban. Such a payment is now never carried and makes
  a block invalid, so the ban fires only for two or more payments that together overdraw: two
  signings.
- **E25 (MEDIUM): catch-up skipped the overdraw purge.** A node that caught up kept pending
  payments that together overdrew their payer, and could propose them so that the ban wiped the
  payer. Catch-up now runs the same pool clean-up as a commit.
- **E26 (MEDIUM): keys were loaded without checking the pair.** A node given halves of two different
  key pairs started and signed under an identity its peers did not list, and the wallet took its
  identity from a field its seal does not cover. The node now derives the public key from the
  private key, compares both halves and runs a sign-and-verify self-test at start. The wallet takes
  its identity from the decrypted key.
- **E27 (MEDIUM): setting the wallet passphrase did not seal an existing plaintext key.** Following
  the documented remedy, setting `GOLEDGER_WALLET_PASSPHRASE`, left the key in plaintext and stopped
  the warning. A plaintext key is now sealed, atomically, whenever a passphrase is available, and the
  warning repeats on every start until then. A review follow-up seals the file a symlinked
  `wallet.json` points to.
- **E28 (MEDIUM): one bad height poisoned the wallet's block counter for good.** The wallet's
  persisted next `BlockID` had no bound and no network scope, so one huge height from its node made
  every later payment name a far-future block that never landed. Reported heights and the counter
  are now bounded, and the counter is scoped to the network. A review follow-up keeps a restart
  after a burst of payments from reusing a block a pending payment names.
- **E30 (MEDIUM): seizure votes were public before they counted.** The admin page sent one vote per
  POST, and `GET /hdls/governance` showed the partial tally, so a watching target could move its
  money before the seizure enacted. Fixed with E4: votes are collected off the network, and only
  complete certificates are pooled and shown. A target that changes its balance every block can
  still outrun a certificate bound to its balance, which is why the Phase 2 freeze is not bound to
  the balance.
- **E31 (MEDIUM): the consensus driver spun forever if signing failed.** A vote that could not be
  signed still counted as progress, so the node's only event-loop goroutine looped forever. E2's
  fail-closed sign log made that reachable. The driver now reports progress only for votes it sent
  and retries the rest on a timer, including, after a review follow-up, a nil precommit the log
  refused.
- **E32 (MEDIUM): a hung backup mount slowly killed the node.** Every commit started a new backup
  goroutine, so a mount that hangs instead of failing pinned one OS thread per block until Go
  aborted the process at 10,000 threads. At most one backup mirror now runs at a time.
- **E33 (LOW, rule): a same-block credit let a banned payer keep its balance.** A payer banned for
  overdrawing that also received a payment in the same block kept its whole balance and name,
  contrary to the documented ban. Fixed with E9: a banned payer is rebuilt from zero, so the ban
  destroys exactly the pre-block balance and same-block credits land.
- **E34 (LOW, rule): one key had several accepted base64 spellings.** In dynamic mode one key could
  hold two validator seats and vote twice without evidence. Only the canonical spelling is now
  accepted, in signatures and in the key fields that create identities; review follow-ups extend
  this to the node config and the wallet.
- **E35 (LOW): late double-signs were never slashed.** A driver's evidence was dropped when its
  height ended by catch-up, and conflicting votes that arrived after a decision were ignored (38 of
  38 in a test). Now that E2 has landed, a driver's evidence is kept when the driver is retired, and
  a late precommit that conflicts with the last commit becomes evidence. **Phase 2 (S6, S10):** such
  evidence freezes the name, and a late double-sign wakes an idle chain.
- **E36 (LOW, plausible): admin votes resolved a typed name through the node, unseen.** The admin
  page resolved a typed name to a key through the wallet's node and signed that key without showing
  it. The reassignment form now shows the resolved key with its registered name and balance before
  anything is signed, and refuses a target that is neither a registered name nor a public key.
- **E37 (LOW): the demo left the founder key in plaintext in `/tmp`.** `cmd/demo` wrote the
  founder's private key to a fixed path and never deleted it. The key now goes into a private
  temporary directory, written with `O_EXCL`, and the directory is removed on exit.
- **E39 (LOW): genesis accepted duplicate keys, non-positive amounts and duplicate names.** A config
  typo could start a network in a state the ledger never produces, and a key listed twice silently
  lost a balance. `load` now refuses non-canonical or duplicate keys, negative amounts, and
  duplicate or overlong names. A review follow-up accepts zero balances again, since named accounts
  at zero are now normal state.
- **E40 (LOW): `/hdls/resolve` answers are unsigned, and the docs' digest cross-check was weak.** A
  node can pair a forged answer with the honest digest, so comparing digests across nodes, as the
  docs advised, did not catch it, and the reported height was off by one. The answer now reports
  the committed height, and the docs say to compare the key and address fields.
- **E41 (LOW): observer nodes got no peers on `maxPeers` networks.** A node whose key is not a
  validator started with zero peers, so it receipted payments it never relayed. It now keeps its
  configured peers.
- **E42 (LOW): accounts created by a grant back no validator, and the wallet could not delegate.**
  The wallet's account form now has a delegate field. Grants are not delegated automatically, since a
  large grant would hand one key a quorum; the docs say so and advise keeping the treasury
  undelegated.

**Fixed in part in Phase 1; finished in Phase 2 (E15 in S4, E16 in S4 and S6)**

- **E15 (MEDIUM): a proposal's signature does not cover its block.** Anyone who sees a genuine
  proposal (a validator, or a LAN sniffer, since gossip is plain HTTP) can attach a junk block to
  its signature, and a node that got the junk copy first stored and relayed it, dropped the genuine
  copy and could not commit the height until a checkpoint catch-up. A node now acts only on a copy
  whose block applies and never replaces a stored valid block. The cost that remains is under the
  limits below. Phase 2 adds a block hash to the signed fields. **Fixed (S4):** a proposal signs
  the block hash (`consensus.SignedProposal`), which covers the whole block, and a node drops a
  copy whose block does not hash to it before `ApplyBlock`.
- **E16 (MEDIUM): two different proposals from one proposer went unnoticed.** A second, different
  proposal for the same round was applied, stored and relayed, and nothing recorded the conflict,
  although it is exactly the "two signings" the burn rule is about. The first proposal per round
  now wins, the conflicting pair is logged for operators, and a node's own proposals are now in its
  sign log (below). Phase 2 carries proposal evidence in blocks. **Fixed (S4, S6):** a second
  authentic proposal for a held round is kept as `ProposalEvidence` (S4 added the type, S6 the
  rule), a block carries it, and it freezes the proposer's name.

**Left for Phase 2, and fixed there**

- **E13 (MEDIUM): a slash takes the balance, not the key.** In static mode a slashed key keeps its
  seat with full power (after its slash, it still cast the deciding ⅔ vote on a 1,000-unit
  reassignment), and in dynamic mode stake delegated by others keeps it seated. In Phase 2 a
  double-sign freezes the validator's name, which takes it out of the set until the ⅔ re-keys it.
  Until then a slash takes the offender's balance, a named offender keeps its name at zero, and the
  docs say so. One incoming payment also re-seats a self-staked offender, which is then not slashed
  again. **Fixed (S6):** a double-sign freezes the name and leaves its balance; a frozen name is out
  of the validator set in both modes until the ⅔ name action re-keys it.
- **E20 (MEDIUM): a re-genesis that keeps the network id replays the old chain.** Signatures bind
  only the network-id string, so on a same-id re-genesis old payments and ⅔ grants were accepted
  again, and one node with an old snapshot pulled every node back onto the old chain within seconds.
  The docs now say that every re-genesis takes a new `networkId` and an empty `snapshotPath`, and
  restores no old backups; a node also refuses a sign log that does not verify on its network.
  Phase 2 derives the network id from the genesis. **Fixed (S4, S8):** the network id is the
  network's name and the genesis hash (`ledger.GenesisHash`, `ledger.NetworkID`), read from the
  genesis file every node shares.
- **E29 (MEDIUM): there is no freeze, and no way to move a name to a new key.** The agreed recovery
  model (the banks freeze, the police decide, a ⅔ vote reassigns) has only its last step, and a
  thief keeps spending while votes are collected. Phase 2 adds a ⅔ freeze and name action, the
  holder's own freeze, and key and bank changes (below). **Fixed (S5, S9):** name requests
  (registration, key change, bank change, freeze) and the ⅔ name action with `NewKey`, `Bank`,
  `Freeze` and `Unfreeze` (S5); the wallet's freeze button, Requests page and name action draft
  (S9).
- **E38 (LOW): the fork-binding pin compares the wrong digest.** A payment's `PreviousStateDigest` is
  compared with an accounts-only digest, while the docs and `/hdls/resolve` gave the full state
  digest, so a pin computed as documented never landed, yet the payer got a receipt. The docs now
  name the accounts-only digest. Phase 2 compares the full digest and checks it at intake.
  **Fixed (S4):** the pin is the full state digest that `/hdls/resolve` serves, and intake takes a
  pin only for the next block and that digest.
- **E44 (INFO): payments have no conflict slot.** One payment drains an account, so the burn rule
  can never catch a thief holding a payment key. Phase 2's holder freeze and ⅔ freeze and name
  action cover it. **Fixed (S5).**

**Documented**

- **E45 (INFO, plausible): validator power saturates at 2^40 units.** Validators above the cap
  count as equal; that is unreachable at community scale. `docs/security.md` now says so.

**Accepted**

- **E43 (INFO): conflicting account updates and governance votes from one key are not detected.**
  Harmless today, so no change. The unused `validator-message` and `server-auth` signing domains
  that the review mentions are gone (Phase 2, S1).

**Refuted**

- The claim that float `math.Pow` and `Ceil` in `GroupParameters` could split nodes on different
  architectures: 1.3 million (participants, `maxPeers`) cases on amd64 and 386 gave identical
  results.

## How Phase 1 was built

Four groups worked in parallel on separate branches: the node event loop, consensus driver and
persistence; the ledger rules and signing; node intake, pools, peer DNS and backup; and the wallet,
`cmd/server` and `cmd/demo`. Each fix came with a regression test in the package's normal test
files, checked to fail without the fix. The four branches were merged with five small integration
commits, whose subjects start with "integrate:", and the full suites of both modules passed under
`-race`.

The order was fixed in advance. The sign log (E2) came before anything that punishes
double-signing, since without it an honest validator that rebooted mid-height produced valid
evidence against itself.

## Adversarial review of the fixes

Three reviewers then attacked the merged result, one each for consensus, the ledger rules, and
intake plus the wallet. An issue counted only with a failing test the reviewer ran or an exact
trace. They confirmed 24 issues: 1 high, 4 medium, 12 low and 7 nits. 23 were fixed, in the 19
commits whose subjects end in "(review follow-up)"; where two reviewers reported the same or a
neighbouring issue, one commit fixes both. Every fix except one dead-code removal came with a new
or extended test that fails without it.

- **High: E12 was not closed for invalid proposals.** First-wins covered only the proposals a node
  took, and since E15 a node takes only blocks that apply. So a round's scheduled proposer could
  re-send one signed junk block, unchanged or with other bodies, and every copy cost every node a
  full `ApplyBlock` (about 360 ms for a 1 MiB body); a validator scheduled for round 0 or 1 could
  keep every honest event loop saturated. "E12: stop checking a round's proposals once its blocks
  keep failing": once two authentic proposals for a round have failed to apply, the node takes no
  more for that round. The better fix, a block hash in the proposal signature, changes a signing
  preimage, so it waits for Phase 2. **Done in Phase 2 (S4).**
- **Medium:**
  - after a failed sign-log write, the nil precommit was never retried, and the height stayed stuck
    after the disk recovered ("E31: retry a nil precommit the sign log refused");
  - after a catch-up or a bootstrap, a power loss before the node's next commit left the sign log
    ahead of the snapshot on disk, and the validator then refused to restart ("E2: save an adopted
    or caught-up checkpoint before voting on it");
  - two wallet account updates sent in one block interval named the same block, and E8's
    one-update-per-account policy silently dropped the second ("E8: give each wallet account update
    a block of its own");
  - any account could still fill the update pool with updates inside the window, so every honest
    update was answered 202 and dropped ("E21: hold one pending update per account and block").
- **Low and nits**, which tightened the edges of the fixes:
  - a sign log that does not verify on the node's network, such as one left over from a re-genesis,
    is refused at start instead of bringing the validator back locked on the old chain's block
    ("E2: refuse a sign log that does not verify on the node's network");
  - a validator that resumes from its logged precommit no longer leaves its peers one precommit
    short ("E2: re-gossip the last commit's precommits to nodes still at that height");
  - the wallet and the admin drafts refuse an amount off the 10⁻⁸ grid before signing it, instead of
    signing what every node refuses ("E7: parse wallet and admin amounts with ledger.ParseAmount");
  - validator keys, a genesis `validatorNode` and the node's own key must be canonical too, which
    also stops a validator from starting without the `snapshotPath` its sign log needs ("E34:
    refuse keys in another base64 spelling anywhere in the node config");
  - a large balance's fraction form could exceed the 64-character amount cap, after which the node
    could not reopen its own snapshot; the cap is now 128 ("E6: raise the amount length cap so every
    reachable balance reads back");
  - a certificate holding one departed voter's vote was refused whole at intake, behind a 202 ("E18:
    set a departed validator's vote aside at intake too");
  - a wallet restart after a burst of payments named blocks that pending payments already named
    ("E28: a restarted wallet keeps the counter a burst of payments left");
  - named accounts at zero, which E9 keeps, could not be carried into a new genesis ("E39: accept
    genesis accounts at zero");
  - a newcomer's account update sent before its first credit was dropped while the wallet reported
    success ("E21: refuse a wallet account update before the account is on the ledger");
  - re-sealing replaced a symlinked `wallet.json` and left the plaintext key at the link's target
    ("E27: seal the file a symlinked wallet.json points to");
  - "E34: the wallet takes a key only in its canonical spelling", "E5: pick the block's governance
    action before its payments", "E19: name the one-day timing ceiling in the config error", and
    the dead-code removal "E6: drop the negative-cost check that ParseAmount made unreachable".
- **Skipped (one nit).** Three small points in the E1 and E2 code. The height check in `commit`,
  which the reviewer called unreachable, stays as a one-line assertion, since it is the commit half
  of the guard against the critical E1. A suggested digest check in the sign log would have been
  dead code. The third point, that a node's own proposal was not in
  its sign log, needed a new record in the log file or a reordering in the driver, and in Phase 1
  its only effect was a log line on other nodes, so it was left for Phase 2's proposal evidence. A
  later check of the Phase 2 freeze rule showed that the same gap lets a proposer whose log write
  fails send two different blocks for one round after a restart. It is now fixed in Phase 1
  (below).

## What came after

**Go 1.27.** "Go 1.27: build on a supported toolchain; take PBKDF2 from the standard library". Go
1.22 had had no security fixes since February 2025, and every node serves `net/http`. Both modules
now require Go 1.27 and name go1.27.3 as their toolchain. The wallet's hand-written
PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256, which existed only because Go 1.22 had no `crypto/pbkdf2`, gives way to the
standard library's, and a wallet sealed by the old code still opens. Go 1.27 also ships
`crypto/mldsa`, which Phase 2 uses.

**The wallet's validator pin.** "Wallet: believe only states that the pinned validators signed". The
wallet took both the state it showed and the list of validators that vouch for receipts from its
node, over plain HTTP, and never checked the commit on a checkpoint. A dishonest node, or anyone in
between, could show a balance the network never committed and list its own key as a validator, so
its self-signed receipt showed as verified. The wallet now pins the validators it trusts in
`<keyfile>.trust`: the list an operator publishes or, without that file, the validators that signed
the first state it saw. It shows a state only if validators holding more than ⅔ of the pinned power
signed it, and verifies receipts only from pinned validators. The height a payment names still
comes from `/hdls/status`; a false one can only make the payment miss its block, and E28 bounds it.
(2026-10-31: a false height could also make a restarted wallet drop its counter and name a used
block again. The height a payment names now comes from the verified checkpoint.)

**".5" amounts.** "Wallet: accept an amount typed with a leading decimal point". Once the wallet used
the network's strict parser, it refused ".5", which people type for 0.5. It now adds the missing 0
before parsing. What it signs is unchanged.

**Three flaky chaos tests were production liveness bugs.**
`TestChaosLossDupReorderKeepsAgreementAndProgress`, `TestChaosDynamicAdmissionConverges` and
`TestChaosEquivocatorIsSlashedWithoutFork` failed now and then; `TODO.md` tracked the admission
test as a starved-runner flake. Each was root-caused under go1.27.3 and go1.26.4 by one agent and
confirmed by an independent verifier
([`results/chaos_root_cause.json`](review/_evidence_2026-10-27/results/chaos_root_cause.json)).
None of the rates depended on the Go version.

- Three of four validators decide a height in about 100 ms. A validator that had lost every copy of
  one decisive message, the proposal or one precommit, could not decide: once the height was
  decided nothing sent the proposal again, and the last commit's precommits went out only on the
  next re-gossip tick, by when the others had usually moved on. Only a checkpoint could free it. The
  test network has no catch-up source, and neither had a production node whose bootstrap peers were
  down. In a scratch reproduction, one validator down plus one that lost a proposal, with a dead
  bootstrap peer, halted a four-validator network for good. "chaos: send the decided proposal with
  the last commit, at commit and on re-gossip" sends both as a node commits and again on every
  re-gossip tick. The loss-and-reorder test went from 3 failures in 400 runs to 0 in 400 under
  go1.27.3, and runs ending with a validator stranded two or more heights behind went from 137 in
  1,300 to 0 in 1,000. The admission test went from 9 failures in 300 runs to 0 in 300, and from 8
  in 1,000 to 0 in 1,000 in the independent check.
- After Phase 1 the equivocator test still failed 1 run in 400, in two modes. One was a straggler
  at the slashing height that never got the commit, which the change above addresses. In the other,
  a node decided on a quorum that counted the double-signer's first precommit before it had
  precommitted itself. The nodes that had seen both of the double-signer's precommits count it out,
  so they needed every honest precommit, and the decider's never came. "chaos: precommit the block
  a node decides before it has precommitted" makes such a node precommit the decided block first.
  The block is already decided, so that vote can complete no other quorum.
  `TestDriverPrecommitsTheBlockItDecides` fails without it.

The cost is traffic: as it commits, and on every re-gossip tick while the next height runs, each
node re-sends the decided block and the commit's precommits to every peer. That roughly doubles
proposal traffic per height and adds about N × |commit| × (N−1) small vote messages per height
across the network (N validators): negligible at four, but growing as N³.

**Sign-log and catch-up follow-ups.** Three commits finish Phase 1:

- "sync: fall back to gossip peers when no bootstrap peer answers". A node whose bootstrap peers
  were all down never asked its gossip peers for a checkpoint, so it stayed behind for good; both
  chaos fixes ran into this. Since E3 every checkpoint is verified against the node's own last
  committed accounts, so any peer is a safe source. The running catch-up loop now asks the gossip
  peers when no bootstrap peer yields a verified checkpoint. `TestCatchUpFallsBackToGossipPeers`
  fails with the old source selection (the node stays at height 1) and passes in about 3 s with the
  fix.
- "E2: log a proposal before sending it (follow-up)". This is the point skipped above. A probe
  showed a proposer whose sign-log write was failing sending its proposal anyway, 7 times, and after
  a restart sending a different block for the same height and round: two signed proposals for one
  round, which Phase 2 makes evidence. The node now writes its own proposal to the sign log,
  fsynced, before sending it, and does not send it if that write fails (its prevote for the block,
  logged just before, can still go out); that round then times out to nil. After a restart at the
  logged height it sends the logged proposal again and never builds a different block for that
  round. `Open` refuses a logged proposal signed for another network or another height. Four new or
  extended tests fail on the parent commit.
- "E2: never sign a slot already voted in, and wait one re-gossip after a start without a sign log
  (follow-up)". A dead disk, or an unreadable log that an operator deletes, brings a validator back
  without its sign log, and Phase 1 slashes vote double-signs. Two gaps made such a double-sign
  possible: the driver counted its own vote, re-gossiped back by peers, without marking that slot as
  voted in, and a validator back from a snapshot signed before any re-gossip could reach it. Now a
  verified vote of the node's own, wherever it comes from, marks its slot and, for a precommit of a
  block, restores the lock. A node that starts past genesis (from its own snapshot, or from a
  checkpoint its bootstrap peers gave it at start) without a sign log for that height signs no vote
  or proposal for one re-gossip interval (`timeoutMs`), then resumes from the votes of its own that
  peers sent back. An ordinary restart between heights waits too; a new network starts at once. The
  new tests fail on the parent commit, and the two node tests passed 200 of 200 runs each under
  eight parallel processes.

With all three, the full suites of both modules passed twice under `-race`, and the 20 restart,
chaos, sign-log and resume tests passed 10 runs each.

## Phase 2: decided, and built

Agreed with the owner during the review: there are no users yet, so breaking changes are allowed
and batched into one new genesis; the smallest fix wins; and rare recovery cases go to the ⅔ vote.
Validators are "banks". A theft victim identifies themselves at their bank, the banks vote (⅔) to
freeze, and after the police decide, a ⅔ vote moves the name and, when possible, the money. There
are no recovery keys.

Besides the Phase 2 parts of the findings above (the height in the ledger state, plain-decimal
amounts, a network id bound to the genesis, a block hash in the proposal signature with proposal
evidence, the ⅔ freeze and name action, the full digest in the pin), Phase 2 adds caps on a single
payment and on one name's balance (S2), and delegation to a name (S3), agreed during the review.
The following were decided after it. Each is built, with the slice named:

- **Signature record.** For `RetainBlocks` heights, every node keeps each block's commit
  certificate, its proposer's signature and the validator set whenever it changes. Governance
  certificates, and every double-sign proof a block carried, are kept for the life of the chain.
  Payments and account updates are never kept. `RetainBlocks` is a ledger parameter, never below
  1000; a vote can lengthen it, but a cut applies only to records written after the vote, so the ⅔
  cannot vote away the record of their own votes. So that the record cannot be walked back against
  today's public balances, the signed block digest mixes in a random value the proposer draws.
  **Built (S4, S7, S7b):** the nonce in the block hash (S4); the record (S7); since S7b it is kept
  in unit files of 100 heights named by the network, and served a unit at a time.
- **ML-DSA-65.** Go 1.27 ships `crypto/mldsa`, and a genesis is the only time the signature scheme
  can change. Keys are 1952 bytes and signatures 3309, against 32 and 64 for Ed25519, so the message
  and block sizes are settled first. Signing is deterministic (`SignDeterministic`): the consensus
  driver assumes a signature is a function of what is signed, and Go's `Sign` adds fresh randomness.
  The same genesis must also stop an idle chain from committing a block every second; otherwise the
  signature record (about 50 GB a month with 4 validators) and vote gossip become the main cost.
  **Built (S1, S10):** ML-DSA-65 is the only scheme (S1); a height starts only when there is work
  (S10).
- **Names are the account identity.** An account is its name, and its key is the name's current
  credential, kept in state. Payments, delegations and validator seats name accounts by name, and
  paying to a raw key goes away. Names that hash the key stay a wallet convention for anonymous and
  prepaid wallets. One spelling per name is still to be settled. **Built (S3):** the builders'
  spelling is 1 to 32 lowercase letters, digits and hyphens; the owner's choice is open (spec §12
  Q1).
- **Registration, key and bank changes, and the self-freeze.** Each account has a bank of record.
  (2026-10-31: not every one. A genesis name, or a name the ⅔ create, may have none (R2), and the
  ⅔ name action can clear a name's bank.) A
  free name, meaning one never registered, is registered by a named requestor, who signs and pays
  the fee; the requestor's bank co-signs, and the new key signs to prove its holder has it. A key
  change needs the name's current key, its bank of record and the new key; a bank change needs the
  current key and the new bank, so no bank can hold its customer hostage. Both pay a fee and take
  effect `ChangeDelayBlocks` later, about a day. During that wait the still-valid key can freeze the
  name: the freeze is free and immediate, cancels pending changes, stops payments both ways, and only
  the ⅔ name action lifts it, always with a new key. A name that holds a validator seat changes key
  or bank only by the ⅔ name action. **Built (S5, S9):** in the ledger and the node (S5) and the
  wallet (S9). A freeze names a block at most 64 ahead, the payments' window (owner, 2026-10-30).
- **One key per bank.** A bank co-signs with the key it votes with, so freezing a compromised bank
  key stops its votes and its co-signatures together. A frozen seated name leaves the validator set,
  with the power delegated to it, until the ⅔ re-keys it. **Built (S3, S5):** the set by name
  leaves frozen names out (S3), and a freeze clears every pending change the frozen name co-signed
  (S5).
- **A double-sign freezes the name, with a one-third cap.** Two signatures by a name's current key
  on different blocks in one slot (the same height, round and type for votes; the same height and
  round for proposals, once proposals sign their block) freeze that name. It then leaves the
  validator set in both modes until the ⅔ name action re-keys it. No list of burned keys is kept:
  keys are fresh and free to generate. Evidence is judged from the pre-block state and its two
  signed messages alone, and it counts as long as the convicted key is the name's current key, with
  no other age limit; this replaces `maxEvidenceAge`. A block's evidence freezes a name only if,
  afterwards, all frozen names together hold under a third of the power of seated and frozen names.
  Without the cap, one honest fault in a bank holding more than ⅔, the setup the docs recommend,
  would hand the chain to the rest. At a third or more, recovery is a re-genesis with new keys and
  a new network id. **Built (S6).**

Two adversarial checks shaped these rules: a five-agent design review of the name and key requests
([`results/new_key_design_review.json`](review/_evidence_2026-10-27/results/new_key_design_review.json)),
and three skeptics who tried to break the double-sign freeze
([`results/name_freeze_check.json`](review/_evidence_2026-10-27/results/name_freeze_check.json)).

## Phase 3: the deployment split

The single Go application stays the testing setup. Deployment is planned as three containers that
can live on separate devices: the fetcher, an edge proxy that faces the network; the engine; and the
signer, which holds the key. The signer must refuse a second, different signature for the same
height, round and type, and persist that before it signs. Gossip routes should be reachable only
from other validators. A bank's desk co-signs through the signer, which serves it only the
co-signature and freeze domains. None of this is built.

(Owner, 2026-10-30: four containers, one per docs area, the wallet included; see `TODO.md`.)

(2026-11-01: built, but for the signer, as a new genesis, from
[`review/phase3_spec.md`](review/phase3_spec.md). What is built:

- **The engine's two ports.** The gossip routes are served on a gossip port that lets in only the
  current validators' IP addresses, read from the chain, and the node config's `allow` entries. The
  engine enforces the list in its own process, as it accepts a connection and after each read.
  Every route is served on an operator port, for the bank's own machines, which is never published.
- **Addresses on chain.** Every name may publish a public address and up to 4 IP addresses, signed
  by its own key, and the genesis fixes one gossip port for the network. Validators gossip to each
  other's IP addresses, and fall back to the next address when the connection to one fails.
- **The fetcher.** `cmd/fetcher` serves the files the engine writes for the public, and the record,
  behind a CDN, and passes payments, updates and name requests on to the engine, under a global cap.
  It holds no key and keeps no state.
- **Pigeon mode.** Bundles of signed messages, exported and imported on the operator port and
  carried by hand; carried checkpoints; a slow-down switch, `minimumStepTimeoutMs`, that lets each
  round wait for a carrier; and a second path, where a stuck validator pulls the others' public
  bundles from their public faces.
- **Containers and the quickstart.** The images, and a compose file that runs four banks on one
  machine.

What remains is the signer: the engine still holds the key. See `TODO.md`.)

## Not fixed or not covered

- **Proposal bodies are not signed yet (E15).** Until Phase 2 adds the block hash, each junk copy
  of a proposal under a copied signature costs a node one `ApplyBlock`, and after two such copies
  for a round the node takes no more proposals for it. So a forger who lands two junk copies ahead
  of the genuine proposal costs that node the round: it prevotes nil and, if the others decide,
  catches up from a checkpoint. One junk copy does not. **Fixed in Phase 2 (S4):** a copy with
  another body fails the block hash before `ApplyBlock`, and the genuine copy is still taken.
- **Heavy message loss is left to catch-up.** At 30% message loss, three times the test's rate, the
  independent check of the equivocator fix still saw rare strandings: a node one honest precommit
  short (3 in 1,320 runs), and a decision that a fourth honest node had not precommitted (7 in
  1,020). None appeared in 2,020 runs at the test's 10%. A stranded node recovers by checkpoint
  catch-up, which now also asks its gossip peers when its bootstrap peers are down. Counting a
  double-signer's precommit toward a quorum would close part of this; it was not done. Since Phase 2
  (S10) an idle node re-gossips nothing, so a node one precommit short gets the last commit once, at
  commit time, and otherwise by checkpoint catch-up.
- **Catch-up still prefers a live bootstrap peer.** A bootstrap peer that answers with a verified
  checkpoint suppresses the gossip-peer fallback even when that peer is behind, and the start-time
  sync asks only the bootstrap peers.
- **Losing the sign log is only partly covered.** The wait after a start without a sign log is a
  best-effort backstop. It recovers only votes that a running peer holds and re-gossips within one
  interval, and it does not apply when a running node fast-forwards by catch-up. A validator
  restored from a lagging backup, or started from an empty data directory whose bootstrap peers do
  not answer (it now catches up from its gossip peers), can still catch up into a height it voted at
  and double-sign there, and Phase 1 slashes vote double-signs (Phase 2, S6: a double-sign freezes
  the name). During the wait a node takes proposals only for rounds 0 and 1, so a proposer that lost
  its log after proposing in round 2 or later can still sign a second block for that round (peers
  log it; Phase 1 does not slash it; Phase 2, S6: it is proposal evidence and freezes the name). The
  wait also costs liveness: every start above height 1 without a log for that height waits one
  `timeoutMs`, ordinary restarts between heights included. (Phase 2, S4: a commit leaves an empty
  log for the next height once the snapshot is on disk, so an ordinary restart between heights
  starts at once; the wait is the `stepTimeoutMs` in force.) Votes recovered from peers are not
  written to the new log, so a lock restored from them is lost at a second restart. Never delete the
  sign log, and restart a validator that lost it only while its peers are up, from its latest
  snapshot or with bootstrap peers that answer; never from an older backup or an empty data
  directory left to catch up on its own. (2026-10-31: a node back without its sign log now waits,
  two re-gossip intervals, at every height it may have signed at, not only the one it starts at:
  after a commit or a catch-up too, up to the highest height at which a vote or proposal of its own
  comes back to it, a bound its sign log keeps across restarts. While it waits it takes back its own
  proposal of any round up to 256, and its first new sign log at a height holds the votes and
  proposals it took back, so a second restart keeps them, and the lock they restore. The wait is
  still best effort, by timing, and recovers only what running peers hold and re-gossip; see
  `docs/signature.md`.)
- **A slashed key keeps voting until Phase 2 (E13).** In static mode it keeps its seat with full
  power; in dynamic mode stake delegated by others keeps it seated, and one incoming payment
  re-seats a self-staked offender, which is then not slashed again. **Fixed in Phase 2 (S6).**
- **Phase 3 is a plan** (Phase 2 is built). Until the split, a node's one HTTP port serves gossip,
  wallet and admin routes alike. The Phase 1 intake fixes bound what anyone on it can do, but there
  is still no TLS or authentication at the transport layer: keep the network off the public
  internet, as `docs/security.md` says. (2026-11-01: Phase 3 is built, but for the signer. The
  gossip routes have a port of their own, behind the allow list; the public routes are behind the
  fetcher and a CDN; and the operator port, which serves every route, is never published. A config
  without a gossip port still serves every route on one port, to anyone, for a trial on one
  machine. What remains: the signer, which would take the key off the networked machine; no TLS on
  the gossip port, where every message is signed and nothing is secret; an allow list that trusts
  each validator's own list, and that someone on the path between two banks can pass; and a flood
  aimed at a bank's published address, which its hosting provider absorbs. See
  `docs/security.md`.)
- **The ⅔ trust model.** Consensus is safe while dishonest validators hold under a third of the
  power, and the ⅔ vote is trusted by design. Validators holding more than ⅔ can change parameters,
  reassign balances, and since Phase 2 (S5) freeze and re-key names. No rule stops a dishonest ⅔;
  the Phase 2 signature record (S7) keeps their certificates where they cannot vote them away. The
  double-sign freeze covers double-signing by under a third of the power.
- **Joiners trust their seed.** A node that starts with no snapshot anchors catch-up on genesis, so
  on a dynamic network a joiner must be seeded with a checkpoint from a bank it trusts. A node whose
  last trusted validators hold a third or less of the power that signed a newer commit (for example
  after changes that replace two thirds or more of the set) needs one too. (2026-10-31: the check
  counts against the power of the node's own last trusted set: a node needs a seed when those of
  its validators that signed the newer commit, under the same keys, hold a third or less of that
  set's power.) A
  wallet without the operator's list trusts the validators that signed the first state it saw.
- **No independent audit.** All of the above was found, fixed and checked by LLM agents. Anyone who
  wants to run go-ledger for real value should first have an experienced cryptography and security
  engineer audit the code and the deployment.
